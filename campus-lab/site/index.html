A campus network that proves it does what it is supposed to — before anyone touches a switch

This is a small hospital-style network: five devices, four kinds of users, and a set of rules about who may talk to whom. The device settings were written by a program from one description of the design, and an analysis tool (Batfish) read those settings back and checked every rule. Nothing on this page was drawn by hand — it is what the checks found. Click a device to see its settings, or click a cell in the table to see why traffic got through or was stopped. Source code.

The network

Computers plug into the two access switches at the bottom. The two distribution switches in the middle are where the rules live and where traffic is routed between groups — there are two so that one can fail. The core router at the top is the way out to the data center and the internet.

carries several user groups on one cable (802.1Q trunk) router-to-router link (routed /31, OSPF)

Who is allowed to talk to whom

Read a row left to right: this kind of device tried to reach that destination. A tick means the network did what the rules say — whether the rule was “let it through” or “keep it out”. In a hospital most rules are “keep it out”, so most cells should say isolated; that is the network working, not failing.

✓ checked — does what the rule says reaches — meant to get through, and does isolated — meant to be kept out, and is

What this is, honestly. A lab design, not a live hospital network. The settings are written in the language of real Cisco Catalyst 9300/9500/8300 switches and routers and were checked by formal analysis of those settings, not by running them on physical hardware. I used AI tooling to help write the code; the design, the things it guards against and the decision of what to check are mine, and I can explain any line of it.  —  Timothy Burch, github.com/tburchj
